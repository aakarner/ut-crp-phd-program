# What Changed and Why: Comprehensive Exam Policy Revision (Draft, September 2026)

This page explains how the [revised draft](index.md) differs from the [policy adopted in July 2025](2025-07-adopted.md), what prompted each change, and what we hope it will accomplish. The draft was first circulated on September 19, 2026 and revised on September 25 after a round of comments from current and past PhD Program Coordinators; changes 9–15 below come from that review. We put this together to provide additional background for GSC members who are less familiar with comprehensive exam policies and procedures. A line-by-line comparison is available as a [side-by-side diff](diff.html), though because the revision reorganizes the document that view overstates the change.

## Background

At the CRP faculty retreat before the fall 2026 semester, comprehensive exam administration was identified as one of four PhD program issues to bring to the GSC this year (the others are master's equivalency, methods requirements and curriculum, and governance). In September 2026 the current and past PhD Program Coordinators reviewed the exam process together. This revision grew out of that review.

Three problems drove it.

**The coordinator carries too much of the exam, including parts outside their expertise.** Under current practice the PhD Program Coordinator ends up managing the whole process for every student: prompting for reading lists, chasing questions, sending the exam, collecting grades, reconciling them, and writing the results letter. Past coordinators have described the grade-collection and results-letter step as one of the heaviest parts of the role. Much of this work concerns the substance of a student's specialization, where the advisor, not the coordinator, is the expert. The group agreed that advisors should own preparation of the exam while the coordinator remains the disinterested administrator, and that the coordinator's role should shift toward coaching advisors through the process rather than running it. The expectation is a net reduction in the coordinator's burden and a better process overall.

**The written policy does not describe what we actually do.** The adopted policy sets three fixed exam periods with calendar deadlines that are generally not enforced. In practice, students schedule when their committee is available. The policy implies exactly four questions, when every recent exam has offered two or three per area. It assigns all question-writing to the committee chair, when for several years the Planning Theory and Research Design instructors have written and graded Essays 1 and 2 for every student. Faculty coming to the exam for the first time, and students reading the handbook, cannot learn the real process from the document. Students have reported polling their peers for guidance on basic steps (how to submit revisions, for example) because nothing was written down.

**The document is hard to use.** Responsibilities are scattered across nine sections. The same role appears under different names ("PhD Program Coordinator," "CRP Program Coordinator"), a body that does not exist ("CRP PhD Committee") is given an approval role, and one section repeats itself nearly verbatim. The checklist for students lists only two of the three exam periods.

The revision keeps the exam itself—four areas, one essay per area, the grade scale, the revision process, the outcome paths—and rewrites the document around who does what and when.

## Changes in substance

Each item below states the change, what prompted it, and the improvement we expect. These are the items that need GSC discussion.

### 1. A "Who Does What" section (new Section 2)

**Change.** Responsibilities are collected in one place, by role: student, advisor (committee chair), committee members, core-course faculty, and PhD Program Coordinator. The organizing principle is stated at the top: the advisor and committee *prepare* the exam and *grade individual essays*; the PhD Program Coordinator *administers* the exam, *synthesizes* the grades, and resolves inconsistencies.

**Why.** This is the direct product of the September review. Current and past coordinators agreed that advisors should take more responsibility for preparing the exam—committee communication, reading lists, soliciting and shaping questions—and that this had worked well in the cases where advisors did so. At the same time, the coordinator should remain the one who sends the exam, collects grades, and communicates results, so that every student faces the same process and a disinterested party stands between the student and the committee. Two suggestions from the review shaped the section: a written procedure, organized by phase, that the coordinator can hand to each advisor; and an enumeration of the steps so the GSC can see where the advisor should lead and where the coordinator's role matters. Section 2 is that document.

**Expected outcome.** An advisor preparing their first student for comps can read one section and know exactly what to do. The coordinator's workload drops on the front end (no more chasing reading lists and questions for every student) while the back end (administration, grade synthesis, results) stays consistent. The GSC can see and debate the division of labor explicitly rather than inferring it.

### 2. Fixed exam periods replaced by a flexible timeline (Section 3)

**Change.** The three exam periods (spring/early summer, late summer/fall, winter) and their calendar deadlines (January 15, February 15, April 15, July 30, December 1, and so on) are removed. In their place: the student, advisor, and coordinator choose a start date, and the steps run backward from it—draft reading lists about three months before, final lists about two months before, questions at least two weeks before. Post-exam windows (four weeks to grade, results within five, four weeks for revisions, two weeks to grade revisions) are unchanged.

**Why.** The fixed periods have not been enforced in years. Students take the exam when it works for them and their committee, usually near the start or end of a semester, and the calendar deadlines in the policy have become a source of confusion rather than structure. This change reflects how scheduling has actually worked over the past several years.

The September 25 revision adjusted the lead times after review: final reading lists are due at least one month before the exam (two months was judged ambitious), committee members comment on draft lists within two weeks, and the point at which the full CRP-GSC sees the lists is now stated (near-final lists about two months out for comment; final lists at least one month out with an invitation to propose questions). Committee members are asked to confirm, when they agree to serve, that they can grade within four weeks of the exam.

**Expected outcome.** The policy describes real practice, so students and new faculty can trust it. The lead times still give committees the notice they need, and the grading window is agreed in advance rather than discovered afterward. Nothing about the exam's length or rigor changes.

### 3. Question structure and sourcing written down (Sections 1, 2, 7)

**Change.** The policy now says what every recent exam has done: each of the four areas carries two or three questions, the student answers one per area, and questions must draw on the student's final reading list. On sourcing: the Planning Theory and Research Design instructors always contribute questions for Areas 1 and 2 and grade those essays for every student, whether or not they sit on the committee; the advisor drafts questions for all four areas with particular responsibility for Areas 3 and 4; committee members who are CRP-GSC members are expected to propose questions in all four areas; external members propose in Areas 3 and/or 4; the coordinator circulates the final reading list to the full CRP-GSC with an invitation to propose questions in any area; and questions are newly written for each student rather than recycled.

**Why.** The adopted policy is silent on the number of questions and vague on who writes them ("the chair is expected to develop questions for each of the four essays"; core-course faculty are "expected to develop questions relevant to the courses taught"). The actual practice was established several years ago, after a past coordinator discovered that exam essays were circulating among students: the theory and research design instructors agreed to always write fresh questions for and grade Essays 1 and 2, whether or not they were on the committee. That practice has been followed since but never written into policy. The review also surfaced a related concern: committee members are not always confident grading the theory essay, and the scope and sourcing of each essay should be clear to all faculty. The exam is the GSC's gateway to the dissertation stage, and the committee's work of approving readings and collectively grading is part of how the GSC teaches.

**Expected outcome.** Everyone knows in advance who is writing what. No one is asked to grade outside their expertise: the theory and research design instructors anchor Essays 1 and 2, and committee members grade the essays in their areas. Question quality and integrity improve because the "always new, always tailored" rule is now policy rather than memory.

### 4. Essays 1 and 2 should connect to the student's area (Sections 1 and 7)

**Change.** New language says that theory and research design questions, while engaging the broad literature of the field, should be written so that they connect to the student's specialization and dissertation interests, and explains why.

**Why.** The review noted that some recent Essay 1 questions appeared to fall within students' areas of specialization rather than general planning theory, and asked whether this was intentional. The coordinator's view, shared by the theory and research design instructors, is that it is a feature: questions that engage planning theory and research design through the student's own area produce writing the student can carry into the dissertation and push them to think about how the field's ideas apply to their work. The adopted policy is silent, which is why the practice looked like drift.

**Expected outcome.** The GSC decides deliberately whether this is what we want, and the policy then says so. If adopted, students get more from the exam; if the GSC prefers purely general theory and methods questions, the language can be changed and the practice corrected.

### 5. Advising norm (Section 2)

**Change.** One sentence: taking on a doctoral advisee means committing to lead that student's comprehensive exam process.

**Why.** The review proposed that the GSC adopt this as a shared norm: part of what a faculty member takes on when bringing on a new PhD advisee is the lead role in that student's comprehensive exam. The shift of preparation to advisors (change 1) only works if advisors accept it as part of the role.

**Expected outcome.** A shared expectation, stated once in the policy, that the coordinator can point to.

### 6. Special Field approval (Section 5)

**Change.** A student-designed specialization is approved by the CRP-GSC, rather than by the "CRP PhD Committee."

**Why.** No body by that name exists. If a smaller group (the coordinator and advisor, for instance) is intended, the GSC should say so.

### 7. Unsatisfactory outcomes consolidated (Section 11)

**Change.** The adopted Section 9 contains two near-duplicate paragraphs describing the paths to a full CRP-GSC deliberation. They are merged into one paragraph with the same outcomes and the same majority-vote requirement for termination.

**Why.** Editorial, but consequential enough to flag: the two paragraphs were confusing to read in a high-stakes situation. Worth a careful read to confirm nothing was lost.

### 8. Results-letter timing made explicit (Section 3)

**Change.** The policy states that the student receives the results letter within five weeks of submitting the essays. The adopted policy implies this only through the checklist.

### 9. Dissertation summary required (Sections 2 and 6)

**Change.** The student must submit a one- to two-page summary of the proposed dissertation research with the final reading lists. The adopted policy and the September 19 draft only encouraged it.

**Why.** Reviewers were unanimous that committees cannot write well-targeted questions, especially for Essay 4, without knowing what the student intends to study, and that a student ready to take the exam should be able to produce two pages on their topic.

**Expected outcome.** Better questions, particularly in Areas 3 and 4, and a head start on the dissertation proposal.

### 10. Core-course faculty review the theory and research design reading lists (Sections 2 and 6)

**Change.** The Planning Theory and Research Design instructors review the corresponding reading lists for every student before the lists are finalized. The September 19 draft said they were "encouraged" to.

**Why.** If these faculty write and grade Essays 1 and 2 for every student (change 3), they need to have seen the lists those essays draw on. Reviewers felt this should be an expectation, not an invitation.

**Expected outcome.** Theory and research design questions that fit the student's actual reading, and fewer surprises at grading.

### 11. Flexibility on who reads Essays 3 and 4 (Sections 2 and 9)

**Change.** CRP-GSC committee members are still expected to grade all four essays and propose questions in all four areas, but the advisor and PhD Program Coordinator may agree to a different division where an external member's expertise is closer to the student's topic than a CRP-GSC member's.

**Why.** Reviewers noted that on some committees the external member is the person closest to the student's specialization, and a rigid rule would waste that expertise or burden a CRP-GSC member with an essay outside their field. External grades remain advisory.

**Expected outcome.** Essays graded by the readers best placed to judge them, without changing who makes the final decision.

### 12. PhD Program Coordinator review of questions (Sections 2 and 7)

**Change.** After the advisor submits the assembled questions, the PhD Program Coordinator reviews them for clarity and scope and may return them with comments. The advisor revises and resubmits before the exam is sent.

**Why.** Reviewers wanted a formal, if seldom used, point of quality control: a place to catch questions that are too thin, unclear, or out of scope before the student sees them. Under the adopted policy the coordinator simply forwarded whatever arrived.

**Expected outcome.** A light check that will rarely change anything but protects students from a poorly framed exam.

### 13. Advisor comments on the results letter (Sections 2 and 9)

**Change.** The PhD Program Coordinator shares the draft results letter with the advisor for comment before sending it to the student. The coordinator still synthesizes the grades and sends the letter.

**Why.** The advisor knows the student and the topic; a second read catches errors and tone problems, especially when the student's field is far from the coordinator's.

**Expected outcome.** More accurate and better-calibrated results letters, with no change to who decides.

### 14. Grade labels (Section 9)

**Change.** The two revision grades are renamed from "pass with minor revisions" and "pass with major revisions" to **minor revisions required** and **major revisions required**. The scale is otherwise unchanged: pass with honors, pass, minor revisions required, major revisions required, unsatisfactory.

**Why.** "Pass with revisions" is contradictory: the revision itself can be graded unsatisfactory, so the essay has not yet passed. Reviewers found the old labels confusing.

**Expected outcome.** Students and readers understand that a revision grade is provisional until the revised essay passes.

### 15. Spatial Analytics added to the specialization list (Section 5)

**Change.** Spatial Analytics is listed as a planning specialization.

**Why.** The CRP-GSC approved this specialization in a recent meeting and it is described in the current handbook, but it had not been added to the specialization lists in the handbook or the exam policy.

## Changes in form

These are editorial and should not need discussion.

- **Title standardized to "PhD Program Coordinator."** The adopted text uses this in most places but also "CRP Program Coordinator." The handbook as a whole uses six labels for what are three or four roles, and the PhD handbook revision notes identify this as the single largest source of student confusion. "CRP Program Director" (head of the CRP program) is a different role and is unchanged.
- **Timeline presented as one table** of lead times relative to the exam date (see change 2).
- **Grading responsibilities presented as a table** (who grades which essay). Content unchanged from the adopted bullets.
- **Sections reordered** so the document follows the exam chronologically: purpose, roles, timeline, committee, specialization, reading lists, questions, exam period, grading, revisions, unsatisfactory outcomes.
- **Typos corrected:** "retake the exam the exam sequence" (twice), "chair comprehensive exam committee chair," "Community and regional Planning," "CH√CKLIST."
- **Checklist updated** to use lead times instead of calendar deadlines, to record the intended exam date, and to list the committee-roster and abstract steps.
- **Status header added** so readers can tell an adopted policy from a draft.
- **"Any CRP-GSC reader"** in Section 11: the trigger for an unsatisfactory-outcome meeting is a CRP-GSC reader's grade, consistent with external grades being advisory.
- **Committee members' review window** for draft reading lists stated as two weeks, replacing "in a timely manner."
- **Graduate School renamed.** References updated to the Office of Graduate and Postdoctoral Studies, the unit's current name; the catalog citation is to the Graduate Catalog.

## Unchanged

The PhD Program Coordinator continues to receive all grades and comments, synthesize them into the results letter, and communicate results to the student. Also unchanged: committee composition (four members, chair from CRP-GSC, at least one external); the four essay areas; the list of specializations; the 10-day exam window and all post-exam windows (grading, results, revisions); the five grade levels (relabeled; see change 14); the one-round-of-revisions rule; the two-week silent-consent rule for revised essays; external members' grades being advisory; and the majority vote of the CRP-GSC required to terminate a student's program.
